## Another round: dot products and orthogonality

This pilot connects to the graph's <a href={graphConceptHref('orthogonality')}>orthogonality node</a>. You only need small-integer arithmetic. There is no timer and no lost lives.

One rule: the dot product of two-dimensional vectors means **multiply matching entries, then add the products**. Two nonzero vectors with zero dot product are perpendicular. We use the ordinary Euclidean dot product here.

<DotProductGame lang="en" />

## What the four dot-product games practice

1. **Build a formula**: place multiplication and addition symbols, then calculate in two steps.
2. **Signs**: apply the same rule with negative entries.
3. **Orthogonality detective**: calculate before deciding; some pairs are perpendicular and some are not.
4. **Complete a vector**: find an integer that makes the dot product zero. Finish with an independent problem without displayed intermediate results.

Do not rush. Explain why entries in matching positions are paired, then start another round. Hint and retry counts describe this round only.

## Limits of the dot-product game

- **Filling blanks does not show explanation**: numeric and symbol answers do not establish geometric understanding. Arrow diagrams and short explanations should follow.
- **Scaffolding arranges your reasoning**: following steps may hide difficulty starting alone. The final problem removes intermediate results; later practice should include delayed recall and unfamiliar questions.
- **Small integers cover little**: fractions, higher dimensions, matrices, proofs and other inner products are outside this pilot and need gradual introduction.
- **Random numbers give limited variation**: numbers and orthogonality vary within fixed templates. Error diagnosis, counterexamples, diagrams and modeling should follow.
- **Interaction has a cost**: difficulty dragging is not necessarily mathematical difficulty. Symbols also support selecting by click and then selecting a blank, including keyboard operation.

Use this to practice and find gaps. Finishing a round does not establish mastery, and there is no reason to grind familiar tasks for points.
