Objective: Reconstruct higher-order Greeks using calculus, unifying changes in multiple risk inputs into a Taylor expansion; extend to numerical issues with IV. Duration approximately 90-120 minutes. Prerequisites: 02, 06, 07.

## 1. Multivariate Price Is Not Just Spot Curvature

First consider only $S,\sigma$ two inputs. Let $s=\delta S$, $v=\delta\sigma$; the price change to second order is

$$
\delta V=\Delta s+\nu v+\frac12\Gamma s^2+V_{S\sigma}sv+\frac12 V_{\sigma\sigma}v^2+O((|s|+|v|)^3).
$$

Here the units of each input are fixed; for strict comparison of 'magnitude', one should first nondimensionalize by their respective scales. There is no $1/2$ before the mixed terms: in the Hessian quadratic form, the two symmetric cross terms each have $1/2$, summing exactly to 1.

Vanna is defined as $V_{S\sigma}=\Delta_\sigma=\nu_S$; Vomma/Volga is defined as $V_{\sigma\sigma}=\nu_\sigma$. Names may vary by software convention; this textbook always uses the partial derivative definitions as the standard.

## 2. Vanna: First Take the sigma Derivative of Delta

In Part 06, we obtained $(d_1)_\sigma=-d_2/\sigma$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Vanna}=e^{-qT}\phi(d_1)(d_1)_\sigma=-\frac{e^{-qT}\phi(d_1)d_2}{\sigma}.
$$

Alternatively, take the derivative of $\nu=A\phi(d_1)\sqrt T$ with respect to $S$, using $\phi'=-d_1\phi$ to get

$$
\operatorname{Vanna}=\frac{\nu}{S}\left(1-\frac{d_1}{\tau}\right).
$$

The two expressions are equal because $\tau-d_1=-d_2$. In smooth regions, mixed partial derivatives are symmetric, providing a second check route.

## 3. Vomma: Taking the sigma derivative of Vega again

In Vega, $A\sqrt T$ does not vary with $\sigma$, so only the density derivative is needed:

$$
\operatorname{Vomma}=\nu\frac{d_1d_2}{\sigma}.
$$

Vega is always positive, but the sign of Vomma is determined by $d_1d_2$, so the price is not necessarily globally convex with respect to volatility. Taking the parameter card from Part 07 as an example, $d_2=0$, both Vanna and Vomma are zero; this is a special point and does not imply that the general value is zero.

## 4. Speed and Zomma: How does Gamma change with respect to inputs?

First take the logarithm of Gamma: $\log\Gamma=-qT+\log\phi(d_1)-\log S-\log\sigma-\log T/2$. Differentiating with respect to $S$ yields Speed:

$$
\operatorname{Speed}=V_{SSS}=-\frac{\Gamma}{S}\left(1+\frac{d_1}{\tau}\right).
$$

Differentiating with respect to $\sigma$ yields Zomma:

$$
\operatorname{Zomma}=V_{SS\sigma}=\frac{\Gamma}{\sigma}(d_1d_2-1).
$$

Speed is the coefficient of the forward Gamma leading error $h f^{(3)}$ in Part 03. Thus, a "higher-order Greek" is also a quantity that determines truncation error in numerical analysis. The CS leading error for Delta is $-h^2\operatorname{Speed}/6$.

## 5. Time-Dependent Greeks: Unified calendar-time notation

Let

$$
g=(d_1)_T=\frac{r-q}{\tau}-\frac{d_2}{2T}.
$$

Charm is defined as the partial derivative of Delta with respect to calendar time $\partial_t\Delta=-\Delta_T$. For Call and Put respectively, we have

$$
\operatorname{Charm}_C=q\Delta_C-e^{-qT}\phi(d_1)g.
$$

$$
\operatorname{Charm}_P=q\Delta_P-e^{-qT}\phi(d_1)g.
$$

The difference between the two is $q e^{-qT}$. This difference can also be obtained by taking the calendar-time derivative of the Delta parity directly.

Color is defined as $\partial_t\Gamma=-\Gamma_T$. Logarithmic differentiation yields

$$
\operatorname{Color}=\Gamma\left(q+\frac1{2T}+d_1g\right).
$$

Veta is defined as $\partial_t\nu=-\nu_T$, similarly

$$
\operatorname{Veta}=\nu\left(q-\frac1{2T}+d_1g\right).
$$

These time derivatives hold the current Spot and other model inputs constant; they do not equal the total change in Delta/Gamma/Vega observed along a random market path. Some references define Charm/Color/Veta using derivatives with respect to remaining maturity, in which case the signs will be opposite to those here.

## 6. Ultima, Dual Greeks, and Elasticity

Let $p=d_1d_2$. From $d_{1,\sigma}=-d_2/\sigma$, $d_{2,\sigma}=-d_1/\sigma$, we obtain $p_\sigma=-(d_1^2+d_2^2)/\sigma$. Differentiating Vomma again:

$$
\operatorname{Ultima}=V_{\sigma\sigma\sigma}=\frac{\nu}{\sigma^2}\left(p(p-1)-d_1^2-d_2^2\right).
$$

Differentiating with respect to the strike $K$ yields Dual Delta, which should not be confused with Spot Delta:

$$
C_K=-e^{-rT}\Phi(d_2),\quad P_K=e^{-rT}\Phi(-d_2).
$$

$$
C_{KK}=P_{KK}=\frac{e^{-rT}\phi(d_2)}{K\sigma\sqrt T}.
$$

Finally, the price elasticity Lambda is $\lambda=S\Delta/V$, representing the local percentage rate of price change. If $V$ is very small, this ratio becomes large and ill-conditioned; do not divide directly when $V=0$.

All these higher-order Greeks involving $\sigma$ or at least second-order $S$ derivatives are identical for Call/Put pairs with the same parameters, because the parity difference $A-B$ is independent of volatility and linear in Spot. Charm is a first-order Spot plus time derivative, and thus retains the Call/Put difference.

## 7. How to numerically verify mixed derivatives?

A central mixed difference that does not require new production interfaces is

$$
V_{S\sigma}\approx\frac{V(S+h,\sigma+k)-V(S+h,\sigma-k)-V(S-h,\sigma+k)+V(S-h,\sigma-k)}{4hk}.
$$

The local truncation error is $O(h^2+k^2)$, and roundoff error can be amplified by $1/(hk)$. One can also cross-check by differencing the analytic Vega in the $S$ direction and the analytic Delta in the $\sigma$ direction; this saves price calls, but if the analytic formulas share a common error, they may still support each other incorrectly.

Do not simply nest the same ordinary complex $i$ CS to compute mixed derivatives: the inner "take imaginary part" step usually destroys the analytic structure required by the outer step. Higher-order CS, multicomplex, and automatic differentiation can serve as follow-up routes, requiring explicit representations and theory.

## 8. Connecting Greeks to model risk and surface inputs

If the volatility input from an external system is itself $\sigma(S)$, the total Spot derivative of the price is not the fixed-volatility Delta:

$$
\frac{dV}{dS}=\Delta+\nu\sigma_S.
$$

$$
\frac{d^2V}{dS^2}=\Gamma+2\operatorname{Vanna}\sigma_S+\operatorname{Vomma}\sigma_S^2+\nu\sigma_{SS}.
$$

This explains why the system should simultaneously specify "Greeks with fixed model inputs" and "risk moving according to a certain volatility surface rule". The formulas earlier in this course are all partial derivatives with other BS inputs fixed.

## 9. IV: Vega determines the local sensitivity of the inverse problem

Given a compliant market price $V_m$, solve for $V(\sigma)=V_m$. Under $S,K,T>0$ and finite positive $\sigma$, Vega is greater than 0, so internally reachable prices correspond to a unique positive IV. Boundary prices may only correspond to the limits of zero volatility or infinite volatility; out-of-bound quotes have no solution under this model.

Differentiating the implicit function yields

$$
\delta\sigma\approx\frac{\delta V_m}{\nu},\quad \frac{d\sigma}{dV_m}=\frac{1}{\nu}.
$$

For deep OTM or very short maturities, Vega can be very small, so tiny quote errors correspond to large IV uncertainty. The Newton update is

$$
\sigma_{n+1}=\sigma_n-\frac{V(\sigma_n)-V_m}{\nu(\sigma_n)}.
$$

Large steps may produce negative volatility, and Vomma changes the curvature of the price with respect to volatility. A reliable implementation should first check no-arbitrage boundaries, establish a bracket, and fall back to bisection if Newton steps go out of bounds or Vega is too small. A small price residual does not automatically guarantee accurate IV: one should also consider the residual divided by Vega, the bracket width, and the IV interval corresponding to bid/ask.
