## 1. Motivation

M0.5.1 established that an architecture only has to compute each conditional probability. The next defect is that the phrase “recurrent gradient” can sound more complicated than its simplest exact case. AR(1) is a scalar linear RNN: its likelihood, gradient multiplier, autocorrelation, and stationary variance can all be written in closed form. This lesson uses that case as the numerical baseline for M1.

## 2. Forward definition

Let $x_t\in\mathbb{R}$ be a scalar time series, let $\phi\in\mathbb{R}$ be a scalar coefficient, and let $\varepsilon_t\in\mathbb{R}$ be independent noise with $\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$, where $\sigma>0$ is scalar. The AR(1) model is

$$
x_t=\phi x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\in\mathbb{R},
\qquad
p(x_t\mid x_{t-1};\phi,\sigma)=\mathcal{N}(\phi x_{t-1},\sigma^2)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}.
$$

Written as a linear RNN with hidden state $h_t\in\mathbb{R}$, input omitted, and $W_h=[\phi]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times1}$,

$$
h_t=W_hh_{t-1}=\phi h_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R},
\qquad
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}}\in\mathbb{R}.
$$

The widget **V-M0.5-a** exposes $\phi$, $\sigma$, horizon, and seed. Its equations are the recurrence above and the closed-form multiplier $\phi^k$ from D-M0.5.2-02.

## 3. Derivations

### D-M0.5.2-01 · Gaussian AR(1) MLE is least squares

**Claim.** With fixed $\sigma>0$ and observations $x_{1:T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$, the maximum-likelihood estimate is

$$
\hat{\phi}=\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_tx_{t-1}}{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^{2}}\in\mathbb{R},
$$

provided the denominator is nonzero.

**Steps.**

1. Write the scalar Gaussian conditional density:
   $$
   p(x_t\mid x_{t-1};\phi,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}.
   $$
   (The AR(1) equation sets the conditional mean and the Gaussian noise sets the variance.)
2. Multiply the conditionals for $t=2,\ldots,T$:
   $$
   \mathcal{L}(\phi)=\prod_{t=2}^{T}p(x_t\mid x_{t-1};\phi,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}.
   $$
   (Independent noise makes the conditional likelihood a product.)
3. Take logarithms:
   $$
   \ell(\phi)=\log\mathcal{L}(\phi)
   =-\frac{T-1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})^2\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The logarithm converts the product into a sum and exposes the squared residuals.)
4. Because $\sigma$ is fixed, the first term is constant in $\phi$:
   $$
   \arg\max_\phi\ell(\phi)=\arg\min_\phi\sum_{t=2}^{T}(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})^2\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (Multiplying by the positive constant $2\sigma^2$ and changing the sign do not change the optimizer.)
5. Differentiate the variable part:
   $$
   \frac{d\ell}{d\phi}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The derivative of $(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})^2$ is $-2x_{t-1}(x_t-\phi x_{t-1})$.)
6. Set the derivative to zero and expand the sum:
   $$
   0=\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}x_t-\phi\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^2\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (A differentiable interior optimum satisfies the scalar normal equation.)
7. Solve for $\phi$:
   $$
   \hat{\phi}=\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_tx_{t-1}}{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The nonzero denominator permits division and gives the corrected lag-one denominator.)

**Check.** `checks/M0.5/D-M0.5.2-01.py` — `phi_hat = 0.4055944055944055`, normal-equation error `3.469e-16`, `passed = True`.

**Intuition.** Gaussian AR(1) maximum likelihood is ordinary least squares on the lagged scalar. The probability model supplies the loss; the closed form supplies a reference answer for gradient descent.

### D-M0.5.2-02 · AR(1) is a linear RNN and its stability is $|\phi|<1$

**Claim.** For the noiseless recurrence $x_t=\phi x_{t-1}$,

$$
\frac{\partial x_t}{\partial x_{t-k}}=\phi^k\in\mathbb{R}.
$$

For the noisy AR(1) process, the stationary condition is $|\phi|<1$, the lag-$k$ autocorrelation is $\rho(k)=\phi^k$, and the stationary variance is $\sigma^2/(1-\phi^2)\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

**Steps.**

1. Identify the scalar RNN transition: $h_t=\phi h_{t-1}$ with $W_h=[\phi]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times1}$. (A linear RNN has no activation Jacobian beyond its recurrent weight.)
2. Differentiate one step:
   $$
   \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}=\phi\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The derivative of the scalar map $h\mapsto\phi h$ is its coefficient.)
3. Apply the chain rule for two steps:
   $$
   \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-2}}
   =\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}}\frac{\partial h_{t-1}}{\partial h_{t-2}}
   =\phi^2\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The local Jacobians multiply along the path.)
4. Repeat the same multiplication for $k$ steps:
   $$
   \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\phi=\phi^k\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (All $k$ local Jacobians are the same scalar.)
5. For the noisy process, let $v_t=\operatorname{Var}(x_t)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$. Independence and zero noise mean give
   $$
   v_t=\operatorname{Var}(\phi x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t)=\phi^2v_{t-1}+\sigma^2\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}.
   $$
   (The covariance term vanishes because $\varepsilon_t$ is independent of the past.)
6. A finite stationary variance $v$ must solve
   $$
   v=\phi^2v+\sigma^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(1-\phi^2)=\sigma^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v=\frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi^2}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.
   $$
   (Solving the fixed-point equation gives a positive finite value only when $1-\phi^2>0$.)
7. The inequality $1-\phi^2>0$ is equivalent to $|\phi|<1$. (Taking square roots of the positive scalar inequality gives the stationarity condition.)
8. Define the lag-$k$ autocovariance $\gamma(k)=\operatorname{Cov}(x_t,x_{t-k})\in\mathbb{R}$. For $k\ge1$,
   $$
   \gamma(k)=\operatorname{Cov}(\phi x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,x_{t-k})=\phi\gamma(k-1)\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The current innovation is independent of an older state.)
9. Starting with $\gamma(0)=v$, recurse to $\gamma(k)=\phi^kv$ and divide by $v>0$:
   $$
   \rho(k)=\frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)}=\phi^k\in[-1,1].
   $$
   (Autocorrelation is normalized autocovariance, so the stationary variance cancels.)
10. Therefore M1's vanishing or exploding gradient is the same scalar formula with $\phi$ replaced by a product of local Jacobians. (The linear case isolates the mechanism before nonlinear gates and matrices are introduced.)

**Check.** `checks/M0.5/D-M0.5.2-02.py` — gradient multiplier `0.20971520000000007`, finite-difference error `2.538e-12`, stationary-variance error `0.0`, unstable variance `2304.3008679515206`, `passed = True`.

**Intuition.** The same number controls two phenomena: $\phi^k$ is how much a state perturbation survives and how much correlation survives. The widget V-M0.5-a makes the shared curve visible while the sign of $\phi$ distinguishes alternating from non-alternating memory.

### D-M0.5.2-03 · AR(p) least squares and Yule–Walker equations

**Claim.** For an order-$p$ autoregression

$$
x_t=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jx_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t\in\mathbb{R},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},
$$

least squares gives the normal equations $X^\top X\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=X^\top y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$, while a stationary zero-mean process gives the Yule–Walker system $\Gamma_p\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1:p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$.

**Steps.**

1. Stack the lagged observations into $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ and targets into $y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ so that $y\approx X\boldsymbol{\phi}$. (Each row contains the $p$ lagged scalar predictors.)
2. Define the residual sum of squares:
   $$
   S(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\|y-X\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_2^2\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}.
   $$
   (The Gaussian fixed-variance likelihood again differs from this objective only by constants and a positive scale.)
3. Differentiate with respect to the vector parameter:
   $$
   \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}S=-2X^\top(y-X\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.
   $$
   (The transpose maps the residual in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ back to the $p$ parameter coordinates.)
4. Set the gradient to zero:
   $$
   X^\top X\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=X^\top y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.
   $$
   (This is the vector normal equation; solving it gives least squares when $X^\top X$ is invertible.)
5. For a stationary zero-mean process define $\gamma(k)=\mathbb{E}[x_tx_{t-k}]\in\mathbb{R}$ and use $\gamma(-k)=\gamma(k)$. (Stationarity makes covariance depend only on lag.)
6. Multiply the AR(p) equation by $x_{t-k}$, take expectations, and use innovation independence for $1\le k\le p$:
   $$
   \gamma(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j\gamma(k-j)\in\mathbb{R}.
   $$
   (The noise term has zero covariance with past states for these positive lags.)
7. Collect the $p$ equations into the Toeplitz system:
   $$
   \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
   \gamma(0)&\gamma(1)&\cdots&\gamma(p-1)\\
   \gamma(1)&\gamma(0)&\cdots&\gamma(p-2)\\
   \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
   \gamma(p-1)&\gamma(p-2)&\cdots&\gamma(0)
   \end{bmatrix}}_{\Gamma_p\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}}
   \boldsymbol{\phi}
   =\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(1)\\\gamma(2)\\\vdots\\\gamma(p)\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1:p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}.
   $$
   (Symmetry of autocovariance creates the Toeplitz entries.)
8. These are the Yule–Walker equations, not a claim that a finite-sample least-squares estimate equals the sample Yule–Walker estimate exactly. (The two estimators use different finite-sample equations even though both target the same stationary AR family.)

**Check.** `checks/M0.5/D-M0.5.2-03.py` — normal-equation error `0.0`, Yule–Walker error `1.388e-17`, `passed = True`.

**Intuition.** AR(1) gives one scalar normal equation. AR(p) turns it into a matrix system, and stationarity turns the covariance version into a structured Toeplitz system.

## 6. Closing

**Counterintuitive question.** If $|\phi|<1$ is needed for a finite stationary variance, why can a smaller $|\phi|$ make long-range learning harder? **Worked answer:** D-M0.5.2-02 gives $\partial x_t/\partial x_{t-k}=\phi^k$. When $|\phi|$ is well below one, the process is stable but the gradient multiplier decays rapidly; when $|\phi|$ exceeds one, the multiplier grows and the variance becomes unstable. Stability and long-range gradient transport are related but different design goals.
